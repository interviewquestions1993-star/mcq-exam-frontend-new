Electricity - Class 10 Science Study Guide

Electric current describes the rate at which charge flows through a conductor. This guide links current, potential difference and resistance through Ohm’s law, then works through series and parallel circuits, electrical power and energy.

Learning Objectives

  • Use current, charge and time to describe charge flow.
  • Apply Ohm’s law and calculate equivalent resistance in simple circuits.
  • Compare how current and potential difference behave in series and parallel circuits.
  • Calculate electrical power and energy with consistent SI units.

Key Concepts

  • I = Q/t; one ampere is one coulomb per second.
  • V = W/Q; one volt is one joule per coulomb.
  • For an ohmic conductor at constant conditions, V = IR.
  • Series resistances add; parallel branches share the same potential difference.
  • Electrical power can be calculated with P = VI, I²R or V²/R.

Current and potential difference

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge: I = Q/t. Its SI unit is the ampere, with 1 A equal to 1 coulomb per second. A closed conducting path is needed for a steady current in a simple circuit.

Potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving charge between those points: V = W/Q. A cell or battery supplies energy to charges, while circuit components transfer electrical energy into other forms.

Key idea: Current measures charge flow per time; potential difference measures energy transferred per charge.

Resistance and Ohm’s law

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current. For an ohmic conductor kept under constant physical conditions, potential difference is proportional to current: V = IR. The resistance is the ratio V/I, measured in ohms.

Resistance depends on the conductor’s material, length, cross-sectional area and temperature. For a uniform wire at constant temperature, a longer wire has greater resistance, while a thicker wire has lower resistance.

Key idea: Use V = IR only when the component behaves ohmically under the stated conditions.

Series and parallel circuits

In a series circuit, the same current passes through each component. Equivalent resistance is the sum of the individual resistances, so adding a resistor in series increases total resistance. The supply potential difference is shared across the components.

In a parallel circuit, each branch has the same potential difference as the supply. Current divides among the branches, and the total current is the sum of branch currents. The equivalent resistance of parallel resistors is lower than the smallest branch resistance.

Key idea: Series: same current. Parallel: same potential difference across each branch.

Power and electrical energy

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer. In a circuit, P = VI. Combining this with Ohm’s law gives P = I²R and P = V²/R for an ohmic resistor. Choose the form that matches the known values and keep units consistent.

Energy transferred over time t is E = Pt. One kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a 1 kW device running for 1 hour; it equals 3.6 million joules. It is a unit of energy, not power.

Key idea: Power is measured in watts; energy is measured in joules or kilowatt-hours.

Common points to check

  • Confusing energy and power, or treating a kilowatt-hour as a unit of power.
  • Applying the series rule to parallel resistors.
  • Changing units inconsistently, especially minutes to seconds or kilo-ohms to ohms.

Sample Multiple Choice Questions

Try each question before opening its explanation. Use the reasoning to check your understanding.

Q1. A 3 Ω resistor is connected across a 12 V supply. What current flows through it?

  • 0.25 A
  • 4 A
  • 9 A
  • 36 A
Explanation: From V = IR, I = V/R = 12/3 = 4 A.

Q2. Two resistors of 2 Ω and 5 Ω are connected in series. What is their equivalent resistance?

  • 2.5 Ω
  • 3 Ω
  • 7 Ω
  • 10 Ω
Explanation: Series resistances add: R = 2 + 5 = 7 Ω.

Q3. Two resistors are connected in parallel across an ideal 12 V source. What is the potential difference across each resistor?

  • 12 V
  • 6 V
  • It depends on each resistance.
  • 0 V
Explanation: Each parallel branch is connected across the same two source terminals, so each has the source potential difference of 12 V.

Q4. A device operates at 10 V and draws 2 A. What is its electrical power at that moment?

  • 5 W
  • 12 W
  • 20 W
  • 200 W
Explanation: Power P = VI = 10 × 2 = 20 W.

Q5. A 60 W lamp runs for 10 s. How much electrical energy does it use?

  • 6 J
  • 60 J
  • 600 J
  • 6000 J
Explanation: Energy E = Pt = 60 W × 10 s = 600 joules.

Descriptive Practice Questions

Q1. Compare current and potential difference in series and parallel circuits.

Model Answer: In series, the current is the same through each component and the supply potential difference is divided among them. In parallel, the potential difference is the same across each branch and the total current is divided among branches, adding to the source current.

Q2. A 4 Ω resistor is connected to a 12 V source. Find its current and power.

Model Answer: Ohm’s law gives I = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A. Power is P = VI = 12 × 3 = 36 W. The same result follows from P = V²/R = 144/4 = 36 W.
