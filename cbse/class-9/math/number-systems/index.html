Number Systems - Class 9 Mathematics Study Guide

The real number system includes rational and irrational numbers. This guide explains how fractions and decimal expansions identify rational numbers, why some roots are irrational, and how to simplify expressions involving surds.

Learning Objectives

  • Classify integers, fractions, terminating decimals and non-terminating decimals.
  • Recognize rational numbers from their decimal expansions.
  • Explain why common square roots such as √2 are irrational.
  • Simplify surds and rationalize simple denominators.

Key Concepts

  • Rational numbers are ratios of integers with a non-zero denominator.
  • A rational decimal terminates or eventually repeats.
  • The square root of a positive non-perfect-square integer is irrational.
  • Simplify radicals by extracting perfect-square factors.
  • Rationalize a denominator by multiplying by a suitable radical expression.

Rational and irrational numbers

A rational number can be written as p/q, where p and q are integers and q is not zero. Integers, terminating decimals and repeating decimals are rational. For instance, 0.125 = 1/8 and 0.333… = 1/3.

An irrational number cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers. Its decimal expansion continues forever without repeating a fixed block. √2 and π are familiar examples. A decimal that has not yet shown a repeating pattern is not automatically irrational; the number itself must be established.

Key idea: A rational number has a terminating or eventually repeating decimal expansion.

Square roots and locating numbers

If a positive integer is not a perfect square, its square root is irrational. To locate √n on a number line, first identify consecutive perfect squares around n. Since 4 < 5 < 9, it follows that 2 < √5 < 3.

This comparison helps estimate a root but does not by itself give an exact decimal value. For calculations, keep an exact expression such as √5 when possible, and use a decimal approximation only when the question asks for one.

Key idea: Use nearby perfect squares to bound a square root before estimating it.

Operations with surds

A surd is an irrational root expression, such as √3. Simplify a square root by taking out perfect-square factors: √12 = √(4 × 3) = 2√3. Like surds can be added or subtracted by combining their coefficients; unlike surds generally cannot be combined.

Rationalizing a denominator rewrites a fraction so that its denominator is rational. For example, multiplying numerator and denominator of 1/√2 by √2 gives √2/2. This changes the form, not the value.

Key idea: Simplify the radical first and combine only like surds.

Properties and careful notation

Rational numbers are closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication. They are also closed under division when the divisor is not zero. The real numbers combine rational and irrational numbers, and arithmetic operations can move an expression between these sets: for example, the sum of a rational and an irrational number is irrational.

When estimating or comparing values, preserve exact forms until the final step. Rounding too early can change a comparison or make an answer appear equal when the exact values differ.

Key idea: Division by zero is undefined; statements about closure must always include this condition.

Common points to check

  • Calling every non-terminating decimal irrational; repeating decimals are rational.
  • Adding unlike surds as if their radical parts were coefficients of the same term.
  • Forgetting that division closure excludes division by zero.

Sample Multiple Choice Questions

Try each question before opening its explanation. Use the reasoning to check your understanding.

Q1. Which fraction is equal to 0.125?

  • 1/4
  • 1/8
  • 1/5
  • 1/12
Explanation: 0.125 = 125/1000. Dividing numerator and denominator by 125 gives 1/8.

Q2. Which statement about √5 is correct?

  • It is rational because 5 is an integer.
  • It lies between 2 and 3 and is irrational.
  • It equals 2.5 exactly.
  • It is an integer.
Explanation: Since 4 < 5 < 9, taking positive square roots gives 2 < √5 < 3. Five is not a perfect square, so √5 is irrational.

Q3. What is the simplest form of √48?

  • 4√3
  • 3√4
  • 2√12
  • 8√6
Explanation: Factor 48 as 16 × 3. Then √48 = √16 × √3 = 4√3.

Q4. Which decimal is rational?

  • 0.1010010001… with no repeating pattern
  • π
  • 0.272727…
  • √7
Explanation: The block 27 repeats, so the decimal is rational. In fact, 0.272727… = 27/99 = 3/11.

Q5. Which is a rationalized form of 1/√3?

  • √3/3
  • 3/√3
  • 1/3
  • √3
Explanation: Multiplying numerator and denominator by √3 gives √3/3. The value is unchanged and the denominator is rational.

Descriptive Practice Questions

Q1. Why is 0.444… rational? Write it as a fraction.

Model Answer: Let x = 0.444…. Then 10x = 4.444…. Subtracting the first equation from the second gives 9x = 4, so x = 4/9. It is therefore a ratio of integers and is rational.

Q2. Simplify √75 and explain the step.

Model Answer: Write 75 = 25 × 3. Then √75 = √25 × √3 = 5√3. The factor 25 is a perfect square, so its square root can be taken outside the radical.
